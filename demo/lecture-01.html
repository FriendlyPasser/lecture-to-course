01 / START WITH THE QUESTION

Among which students?

A survey records 100 students. Forty take statistics; 24 of those also take economics. The question is: among statistics students, what fraction take economics? Before calculating, decide who is eligible to be counted.

Lecture 1 · PDF p. 1

“Among statistics students” restricts the to the 40 students in S. Twenty-four of these 40 meet the condition we are measuring. So the answer is 24 / 40 = 0.60, or 60%.

Think of filtering a list to S before computing a percentage. The excluded students still exist; they simply do not belong in this question's denominator. This is a way of organizing the count, not a claim that taking statistics causes a student to take economics.

The survey case, next step: We can count 24 out of 40, but how could we express the same restriction for any pair of events, even when given probabilities instead of counts? Conditional probability gives that counting idea a general formula.

02 / GIVE THE IDEA A FORMULA

The formula keeps track of the group.

measures the probability of an event within a specified event. Let E mean economics and S mean statistics. Read P(E | S) as “the probability of E, given S”; the event after the bar sets the group. E ∩ S means that both events occur.

To use the formula, P(S) must be greater than zero. A group with zero probability cannot be used as this formula's denominator. The survey has P(S) = 40/100, so the condition is satisfied.

Worked example: words → numbers → symbols

Read each row across to connect the question to the formula. E and S name events, not counts; P gives a probability with no units.

The same survey in three forms
Plain language Numbers and their origin Symbols
All surveyed students 100: the survey total The whole reference set
Take both economics and statistics 24 of 100: the overlap shown in the survey P(E ∩ S) = 24/100
Take statistics 40 of 100: the eligible group P(S) = 40/100
Take economics among statistics students 24 of 40: keep the same overlap, change the reference group P(E | S) = 24/40

Interpretation assumption: choose one of these 100 surveyed students uniformly, so every student has the same chance of selection. Treat the recorded counts as exact for this calculation; they do not establish the probability for every future student.

The equality below is the definition of conditional probability, valid when P(S) > 0. Cancelling the common total is exact algebra, and 24/40 = 0.60 = 60% is exact here. No independence assumption or rounding is used.

Source formula and condition · p. 2
P(E|S)= P(E∩S)P(S)

Take the share in both events and divide by the share in the conditioning event.

  1. Count both: 24/100 = 0.24 of the whole survey take both subjects.
  2. Count the eligible group: 40/100 = 0.40 take statistics.
  3. Rescale to that group: (24/100) / (40/100) = 24/40 = 0.60. The common total of 100 cancels.
Source formula and calculation · p. 2

Conditional: given S

60%

24 out of 40 statistics students also take economics.

Joint: E and S

24%

24 out of all 100 students take both subjects.

Common mistake: the same 24 students appear in both numerators, but the reference groups differ. These answers can both be correct.

Check the condition: If nobody in the survey takes statistics, can this formula return P(E | S) = 0?

Check when the formula applies

No. Both counts would be zero, giving 0/0. This formula is undefined when P(S) = 0; that is different from a defined probability of zero.

Try independently: choose the group in a new setting

Author-created exercise · Another library, with new counts.

A different library has 90 books. Of 21 borrowed books, 7 need repair. One borrowed book is selected uniformly at random. What is the probability that it needs repair? Set up the calculation yourself, then round the probability to three decimal places.

Let R mean needing repair and B mean borrowed. We restrict attention to 21 borrowed books, of which 7 need repair: P(R | B) = (7/90)/(21/90) = 7/21 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333. The fractions are exact; only the final decimal is rounded. P(B) = 21/90 > 0, and uniform selection makes the count ratio appropriate.

Using 7/90 would answer the joint question about all books. Using 21/90 would measure the borrowed share. Neither answers the question within borrowed books.

Optional background: does “given” mean “caused by”?
Supplementary explanation

Conditioning describes a probability within a group. It does not by itself establish a causal effect. The calculation says nothing about what would happen if we made a student take statistics.
