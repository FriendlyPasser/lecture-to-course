Three of twelve counters are blue. What percentage of the counters is blue?

Divide the part by the whole: 3/12 = 0.25 = 25%. Multiplying 3 by 12 gives 36, not a share; 75% describes the nine counters that are not blue.

In the author-created counter example, the numerator counts the three blue counters. The denominator counts all twelve counters in the chosen group. Divide 3 by 12 to get 0.25, then multiply by 100 to write 25%.

Six people read, four swim, and two do both. How many belong to the intersection of readers and swimmers?

The intersection contains the two people who do both. Adding 6 and 4 counts those two twice; 8 counts everyone who does at least one activity.

Refresher: an intersection means both
Supplementary explanation

In the author-created activity example, label the reader group R and the swimmer group W. R ∩ W keeps only people satisfying both descriptions, so its count is 2. It is not the sum of the two group sizes.

In the survey lesson, E ∩ S will likewise mean students taking both economics and statistics.

01 / START WITH THE QUESTION

Among which students?

A survey records 100 students. Forty take statistics; 24 of those also take economics. The question is: among statistics students, what fraction take economics? Before calculating, decide who is eligible to be counted.

Lecture 1 · PDF p. 1

“Among statistics students” restricts the to the 40 students in S. Twenty-four of these 40 meet the condition we are measuring. So the answer is 24 / 40 = 0.60, or 60%.

Think of filtering a list to S before computing a percentage. The excluded students still exist; they simply do not belong in this question's denominator. This is a way of organizing the count, not a claim that taking statistics causes a student to take economics.

Explain it: Why would dividing by 100 answer a different question?

Compare your explanation

Dividing by 100 measures the share of the entire survey taking both subjects. Dividing by 40 measures the share taking economics within the statistics group. Name the group before naming its size.

Reasoning · p. 2

The survey case, next step: We can count 24 out of 40, but how could we express the same restriction for any pair of events, even when given probabilities instead of counts? Conditional probability gives that counting idea a general formula.

02 / GIVE THE IDEA A FORMULA

The formula keeps track of the group.

measures the probability of an event within a specified event. Let E mean economics and S mean statistics. Read P(E | S) as “the probability of E, given S”; the event after the bar sets the group. E ∩ S means that both events occur.

To use the formula, P(S) must be greater than zero. A group with zero probability cannot be used as this formula's denominator. The survey has P(S) = 40/100, so the condition is satisfied.

P(E|S)= P(E∩S)P(S)

Take the share in both events and divide by the share in the conditioning event.

  1. Count both: 24/100 = 0.24 of the whole survey take both subjects.
  2. Count the eligible group: 40/100 = 0.40 take statistics.
  3. Rescale to that group: (24/100) / (40/100) = 24/40 = 0.60. The common total of 100 cancels.
Source formula and calculation · p. 2

Conditional: given S

60%

24 out of 40 statistics students also take economics.

Joint: E and S

24%

24 out of all 100 students take both subjects.

Common mistake: the same 24 students appear in both numerators, but the reference groups differ. These answers can both be correct.

Check the condition: If nobody in the survey takes statistics, can this formula return P(E | S) = 0?

Check when the formula applies

No. Both counts would be zero, giving 0/0. This formula is undefined when P(S) = 0; that is different from a defined probability of zero.

Positive-denominator condition · p. 2

The survey case, next step: The formula explains why 60% and 24% answer different questions about the same students. Can you now choose the group without being shown the steps? Try the survey, then carry the reasoning to a new setting.

03 / APPLY THE IDEA

Choose the group before the arithmetic.

What is P(E | S) in this survey?

Conditioning on S limits the group to 40 statistics students. Of those, 24 take economics. The first choice is the joint probability; the third is the probability of statistics alone.

Author-created exercise · A new situation using the same rule.

A library has 80 books. Twenty are borrowed, and 5 of the borrowed books are damaged. Among borrowed books, what fraction are damaged?

“Among borrowed books” selects 20 books; 5 are damaged, so 5/20 = 25%. The first choice uses the entire library. The second measures the share borrowed; its percentage happens to match the correct answer, but its reasoning answers a different question.

Underlying rule · p. 2

Before moving on, explain the difference between “both” and “given,” state the denominator condition, and solve the library question without copying the survey's numbers.

One question remains in our survey: Does knowing that a student takes statistics change the probability that they take economics? Our 60% answer alone cannot tell us. We need the overall economics share as a comparison. The next lecture supplies that count and uses it to introduce independence.

Optional background: does “given” mean “caused by”?
Supplementary explanation

Conditioning describes a probability within a group. It does not by itself establish a causal effect. The calculation says nothing about what would happen if we made a student take statistics.
