How can probabilities recover the same answer?

  • Recover 4/10 from probability shares and explain why the conditioning probability must be positive.

The club exercise gave 4/10 by counting within the chess group. Keep the same group while rewriting both counts as shares of all 40 members.

Let C mean playing chess and T mean playing tennis. The club counts stay fixed: P(C) = 10/40 and P(T ∩ C) = 4/40.

Worked example: words → numbers → symbols

C and T name events, not counts. P gives a probability with no units; the event after | selects the reference group.

The same club in three forms
Plain language Numbers and their origin Symbols
All club members 40: the club total The whole reference set
Play both tennis and chess 4 of 40: the stated overlap P(T ∩ C) = 4/40
Play chess 10 of 40: the eligible group P(C) = 10/40
Play tennis among chess players 4 of 10: the same overlap within chess players P(T | C) = 4/10

Interpretation assumption: select a club member uniformly, giving each member the same chance. Treat the stated counts as exact; they do not predict every future member.

The following equality is the definition of conditional probability when P(C) > 0. Cancelling 40 is exact algebra, and 4/10 = 0.4 = 40% is exact. No independence assumption or rounding is needed.

Underlying rule · page 2

P(T | C) = P(T ∩ C) / P(C) = (4/40) / (10/40) = 4/10 = 40%.

The common total of 40 cancels. The formula rescales the share doing both activities to the chess group.

This formula requires P(C) > 0. Here 10/40 is positive; if nobody played chess, this ratio would be undefined.

Goal: explain how the overlap changes a conditional probability while its reference group stays fixed.

A means playing tennis; B means playing chess. Keep 40 club members, 16 tennis players and 10 chess players fixed. Select a member uniformly, so everyone is equally likely. Hypothetically rearrange who does both, from 0 to 10 members; all row and column totals stay fixed.

Initially, four of the ten chess players play tennis, so P(A | B) = 4/10 = 40%. At a hypothetical overlap of six, P(A | B) = 6/10 = 60%. The numerator changes; the positive denominator remains 10.

The four cells begin at 4 doing both, 6 chess-only, 12 tennis-only and 18 doing neither. At overlap six, they become 6, 4, 10 and 20. Moving two tennis players into the chess group means moving two tennis players out of the non-chess group, keeping both group totals fixed.

The whole-club tennis probability P(A) remains 16/40 = 40%. It answers a different question from the tennis probability within chess players. At overlap 0 or 10, the latter becomes 0% or 100%; its denominator never becomes zero. These probabilities are exact for the stated counts.

Try independently: a new garden problem

Author-created exercise · A new setting using the same rule.

A garden has 45 plants. Of the 15 plants in shade, 5 are flowering. One shaded plant is selected uniformly at random. What is the probability that it is flowering? Set up the calculation yourself, then round the probability to three decimal places.

Let F mean flowering and H mean in shade. Restrict attention to 15 shaded plants; 5 are flowering. P(F | H) = (5/45)/(15/45) = 5/15 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333. The fractions are exact; only the final decimal is rounded. P(H) = 15/45 > 0, and uniform selection makes the count ratio appropriate.

Using 5/45 would give the joint share of shaded, flowering plants in the whole garden. Using 15/45 would give the shaded share. Neither answers the question within shaded plants.
